To a bored kid,

Recently we were talking about your school experience and that you were frustrated that you couldn't access course material on a wider range of topics. It got me thinking. From what I picked up in our conversations, school is boring and seems to be constructed out of these awkward games that aren’t designed for you -- the games are designed by someone else, for someone else. That can be frustrating and demoralizing. You wait for the nights and weekends when you can explore and play, or so you can just recover from the mental/emotional burden of dragging your mind through the school system you inhabit.

There are two things happening here. First, the interest vs. effort tension. And second, finding interest from within the material.

Interest vs. Effort. You are waiting for interest to drive effort, but the real skill is leveraging effort to drive interest. Here’s an example:

Assignment: read paragraphs about science concepts, memorize vocab for quiz.

Under the “interest drives effort” model, you are supposed to wait for something to be interesting to you, and then you will apply your effort to it. This is doomed to fail for this assignment since the paragraphs are boring and the quiz is not inherently motivating, and you therefore have no reason to apply any effort. What’s the point, after all?

“Effort drives interest” modeling allows you to run a checklist on any assignment or task at school:
1. What skill or knowledge is the teacher actually targeting?
2. What weird, contested, or personal angle on this topic would I genuinely want to know about?
3. What medium would I enjoy producing it in -- code, game, simulation, comic, video, essay?
4. What’s a one-hour prototype I could build today that proves the concept?

If you address these four questions in sequence, and it should take you less than 10-15 minutes, then you can expect to have a personal vision for manufacturing interest. You will have never-ending interesting encounters.

The other trick you can use is to form your judgment on interest from within the material. This is a life-long skill you can develop -- always dive in, give it a try, understand the landscape and some of the nuances of an idea space before you reject it as uninteresting. Anything can be interesting if you can figure out why and how for you personally.

This is a bit like trying the food before you decide you don’t prefer it and want to order something else. Or making it a rule to read the first 50 pages of a book before deciding you aren’t going to finish it. As before, run through a checklist:
1. What is the main idea in this field of knowledge? What are the people in this domain trying to do, prove, or change? (e.g. Biology is trying to articulate the mechanisms that shape life and all its systems. Scientists often seem to be trying understand how evolution works, how DNA and other cellular machines work, and how to manipulate those things for profit (drug companies) or for improving human life (also drug companies, but lots of other kinds of biotech firms that might invent things to do with nutrition, agriculture, bio-augmentation and so on.)
2. What is the weirdest thing about this field of knowledge? (E.g. staying with Biology, I’m sure there are some weird things happening in the field of alien biology -- the search for non-organic compounds on other planets or moons that meet the criteria for life --or perhaps there are just interesting discoveries being made on the extremes, like in the deepest parts of the ocean. I find brain-computer interfaces pretty weird -- like that monkey playing pong just by using a Neuralink chip. I find insects pretty weird in general and there are so many kinds of them. I think it’s weird that animals have language and we haven’t really understood it yet -- though people are using AI to figure out whale speech patterns, maybe there is a big unlock coming soon. Also, there are a bunch of neurons in your gut near your stomach and that I find really weird.)
3. What are the unsolved problems here? (e.g. How and why does life end? Why not extend it indefinitely? Why does sleep matter? How do brains work? Can we optimize bodies or augment them using artificial means? How do we cure cancer? Could we create a universal vaccine?)
4. What does “progress” look like in this domain? (e.g. It seems like we unlock new powers when we figure out new mechanisms -- progress is made when we fight against disease or protect ecosystems, or when we discover new ecosystems. It’s often difficult to predict where progress will occur in science, which is why you want people to just explore and see what they find.)

That’s just me making things up about Biology -- I’m sure you’d get different answers from a professional biologist. But the idea is that you can find weird, interesting features of anything if you go looking for them, and then you will not be bored when learning about those things. You’ll be right at the edge of discovery along with everyone else.

I hope these two checklists help you reframe what it means to be a student. Since being a student is what you will be for your entire life, it will pay off to set this mental framework for yourself now. There will always be something new to learn. Mastering “how to learn” can motivate and inspire you, and you don’t have to wait for anyone’s permission or instruction.

In fact, never let anyone restrict your ability to learn, even if you have to subvert the educational system itself. School is not important on its own; learning is.
